ARTEYE

Sydney

A market analysis of the record nobody is keeping

August 2026 Sydney · Australia · Network Confidential working document
00Executive
summary

Six findings, one verdict

ART EYE is not a listings app that added social features. It is a memory graph for exhibition-going — the Letterboxd pattern applied to visual art — that needs a listings layer to function.

That distinction decides almost everything downstream: who the competitors are, why the moat is durable, and why the obvious revenue model is the wrong one to chase first.

  • The demand-side market is real but thin in one city. Roughly 1.1m Sydney adults attend a visual-arts exhibition in a given year; only ~165k are frequent enough to want a log. A realistic three-year capture is 3,000–10,000 registered curators. That is a community, not a market — and exactly the right size to prove the mechanic.

  • The supply-side revenue ceiling in Sydney is ~A$100k/year. 143 venues, of which perhaps 85 have any marketing budget, against a gallery sector whose flagship fair has seen sales fall from A$23m to A$16m in four years. Monetising Sydney venues is a rounding error that costs the product its neutrality.

  • The defensible asset is the register, not the app. 143 verified venues with coordinates, date-stamped hours, websites, Instagram handles and a self-validating freshness pipeline is eighteen months of work no competitor can generate in a weekend. Everything else in the product is reproducible; this is not.

  • The real competitor is Instagram, not Art Guide. Nobody chooses between ART EYE and Ocula. They choose between opening ART EYE and scrolling the gallery accounts they already follow. Position against that behaviour's failure mode: you saw it, you forgot it, you have no record.

  • Value accrues at network scale. Letterboxd went from 1.7m members (2020) to 30m+ (2026), and from a ~US$50–60m valuation to a reported ~US$250m ask — on a category with the same structure as this one. The visual-arts version is a multi-city network, not a Sydney utility.

  • The largest near-term risk is operational, not competitive. A rebuild of the published site without Supabase credentials silently reverts it to demo mode and makes every account local-only and disposable. One mis-run build destroys the exact asset the strategy depends on.

Verdict

Run Sydney as a density experiment, not a revenue experiment. The gate is not “can we charge galleries?” — it is “do 500 people in one city log three visits a quarter and follow five people?”

If yes, the Melbourne clone is a template and the category is a network. If no, ART EYE is a beautifully-made city guide and should be run as one, at near-zero cost, indefinitely.

Two things a competitor would want, and one liability. The pipeline never writes autonomously — every proposal lands in a human approval inbox, the correct architecture for a trust product in a sector acutely sensitive to being scraped or automated over. And imagery ethics are codified: exhibition covers show the show, never the building; press images come from the venue’s own sharing image with provenance recorded; nothing is hotlinked. In a sector where image rights are the most common reason a gallery refuses a partner, that is a commercial asset disguised as a style rule.

The liability: distribution is a static web export, so push notifications — the only mechanic that reliably converts want to see into went — are inert.

02Market
definition

Four markets, one empty quadrant

ART EYE is mispriced if measured in only one market. It sits between social cataloguing (unoccupied for visual art, in any city), city cultural discovery (crowded, ad-funded and consolidating — Concrete Playground was acquired by Vinyl Group for up to A$5.5m), art-world commerce (Ocula, Artsy — they monetise transactions), and venue audience tooling (the highest-value gap, and the one nobody in Australian visual arts serves well).

03Sizing

Bottom-up, and honest about the ceiling

TAM · Sydney 1.1m Adults 15+ attending a visual-arts or craft exhibition at least once a year (~4.5m adults × ~25% attendance rate).
SAM · Sydney 165k Frequent attenders — three or more visits a year, smartphone-first, actively looking for what’s on.
SOM · 3 years 3.3–10k Registered curators at 2–6% capture; 1.5k–4k monthly actives. Acquisition, not population, is the binding constraint.

Sensitivity. At a 20% attendance rate the TAM is 900k; at 30% it is 1.35m. The capture band barely moves.

Tourism overlay. Sydney drew 3.6m international visitors in the year to June 2025 — 84.6m nights, A$12.7bn spent — and NSW 63.6m domestic visitors. Cultural tourists are high-intent and zero-retention: valuable for session volume and venue-facing reach, worthless for the social graph. Treat them as an argument for the web surface, never as a MAU strategy.

The ceiling, stated plainly

Sydney at 40% conversion and A$1,200 ARPA is ~A$41k ARR; at total saturation, ~A$102k. Nationally, ~A$190k including campaign placement, plus ~A$44k of consumer membership at 50k registered users. A fully-executed ART-EYE-as-listings-business tops out around A$250–500k ARR — an excellent one-person business and a poor venture case. The venture-shaped outcome is reachable only through the network path.

04Demand
side

Who actually uses this

Macro conditions are the best since the survey series began: 74% of Australians attended a live arts event in the past year — 15.4m people, a record — and 98% engage with the arts in some form. Two counter-signals matter more for product decisions than the headline.

  • Cost is now the dominant barrier — 60% cite it, and over half missed something they wanted to see. Gallery-going is the cheapest serious cultural consumption in the country: most commercial galleries and ARIs are free. ART EYE is a free-culture discovery surface at exactly the moment cost is pushing audiences out of ticketed art forms. This is the strongest available tailwind and it is currently unstated in the positioning.
  • Visual arts attendance remains below pre-COVID levels even as live arts hit records. The category is under-served, not over-served — opportunity, but demand must be created rather than merely captured.
  • Cultural-identity participation rose from 32% to 40% between 2022 and 2025, and one in three Australians attended a First Nations arts event. The fairs register already carries the events that serve this. Under-exploited.

05Supply
side

The venue market, and why reach is the wrong thing to sell

This is a two-sided market with an unusually favourable asymmetry: the supply side has already been acquired without its participation. All 143 venues are listed, accurate and live. Venues opt in to control, not in to exist — which inverts the classic cold-start problem. There is no empty marketplace here, only an unclaimed one.

Why “reach” is the wrong pitch in 2026

Sydney Contemporary, the sector’s bellwether, turned over A$16m in 2025 on record attendance of 26,440 — a fourth consecutive year of falling sales. Read that carefully: more people, less money.

07Five
forces

Structural attractiveness

ForceIntensityAnalysis
Threat of substitutesHighInstagram plus Google Maps plus a group chat does 70% of the job for free with no new habit. The 30% it cannot do must be made visible fast, or inertia wins.
Buyer power — venuesHighFree listings exist everywhere, budgets are shrinking, and no venue needs ART EYE in year one. Mitigation: never sell reach; keep listings free forever.
Buyer power — consumersMedium → LowZero switching cost at signup, but every logged visit raises the exit cost. After ~15 logs the user holds a record that exists nowhere else. Retention is a function of log depth — instrument it.
Supplier power — contentMediumVenues own the images and the facts. Mitigated by provenance-tracked sourcing, Wikimedia-licensed venue photos, per-show placeholders, and a pipeline that proposes rather than takes.
Threat of new entrantsMediumTrivial to build the app; hard to build the register and earn the sector’s trust. A funded entrant — a Time Out vertical, an Art Guide product hire — is the credible scenario, against a 12–18 month head start.
Competitive rivalryLowNobody competes for “log what you saw.” The category is uncontested; the attention is not.

Net: attractive on rivalry and entrant risk, hostile on substitutes and buyer power — the profile of a product that wins on habit formation, not on features or price.

The sequencing argument

The instinct is to monetise the 143 venues because they are there. The maths says the entire Sydney venue market is worth less than a single institutional partnership — and charging early costs you the thing that makes the partnership possible: being the neutral, complete record of what is on in Sydney. Completeness is the product. A pay-to-appear agenda is not a record; it is a directory. Grants → institutional partnership → venue insights → consumer membership.

10Unit
economics

The cheapest possible way to be patient

Supabase, GitHub Pages, Expo tooling and an enrichment pipeline hard-capped at thirty model calls per run put the estimated run cost at A$50–200 a month at current scale. This is the most under-rated fact in the analysis: the bear case is survivable indefinitely, so time is an ally rather than a burn-rate enemy. Costs that appear with scale are a developer account, a paid database tier, push infrastructure — and the real one, editorial time, which the proposal pipeline compresses but never eliminates.

11Scenarios

Three futures, one gate

Scenario A · ~50%

The Sydney Utility

A$30–60k / yr

Best register in the city, 2–4k registered users, social graph never reaches liquidity. Revenue from one or two institutional partnerships and a few venue subscriptions. Near-zero cost, indefinitely sustainable, one person. Not a failure — durable, respected Sydney cultural infrastructure, entirely owner-controlled.

Scenario B · ~30%

The National Guide

A$150–250k ARR

Melbourne and Brisbane cloned from the Sydney template; 15–25k registered; the insights tier converts nationally. Requires a second person on editorial and partnerships, native apps in both stores, and a repeatable city-launch playbook. Competes head-on with Art Guide and must win on product, not coverage.

Scenario C · ~20%

The Network

Venture-shaped

Liquidity proven in Sydney, the mechanic travels, and the product goes global-city: London, New York, Berlin, Tokyo, Seoul, Mexico City. The comparable is explicit — Letterboxd at ~US$50–60m in 2023 and a reported ~US$250m ask in 2026. Visual art is a smaller category than film, but exhibitions are time-boxed and local, which makes the data more valuable per user, not less.

The gate between A/B and C is the 500-logger liquidity test, measured within nine months of a native launch. Do not fund a Melbourne launch before it passes.

15Method

Assumptions, and what this does not claim

Sizing is bottom-up from population and published attendance rates. Competitive analysis draws on public positioning and business models. Supply-side counts come from ART EYE’s own register — 143 distinct venue slugs. Financial models are built from stated benchmarks and marked as estimates throughout.

Assumptions requiring validation, in order of leverage

  • 25% visual-arts attendance rate. From the National Arts Participation Survey series. The 2025 wave’s artform-level figure was not directly retrievable at time of writing. Sensitivity tested at 20–30%; the capture band is insensitive to it.
  • 15% of attenders are frequent (3+ visits a year). An inference from category behaviour, not a measurement. It sets the SAM directly — the highest-value assumption to test, and testable cheaply from the app’s own logs once ~200 users exist.
  • A$1,200 annual venue ARPA. Inferred from small-business software norms and sector budget pressure. No price has been put to any venue.
  • ~85 revenue-addressable Sydney venues. Segmentation of the register by type and commercial status; directionally sound, not audited line by line.
  • Greater Sydney ~5.5m, adults 15+ ~82%. Standard population estimates.
What this analysis does not claim

Any figure for ART EYE’s current users, sessions or retention. No analytics data exists in the repository. Every user-side number here is a market estimate, not a measurement — and the first recommended action is to make that untrue.
